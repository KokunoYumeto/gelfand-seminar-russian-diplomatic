## Findings by record

### 69: fixed points and distributions

Bott's near-contemporary Bourbaki exposition supplies an unusually close match to the fixed-point argument, including regularization and the transverse-distribution calculation (Bott, 1966, pp. 265–270, Exposé 295). The lecture was delivered in November 1965 and cites the Harvard notes from 1964; it therefore does not support a claim that this was a new theorem originating in the notebook talk. The modern statement requires a finite elliptic complex over a compact boundaryless manifold and a geometric chain endomorphism with isolated nondegenerate fixed points. For the real local fixed-point formula the denominator is the **absolute value** of the determinant of \(I-df\). Finite-dimensional cohomological traces, regularized operator traces, and the restriction of a distributional kernel to the diagonal must be kept distinct.

The notes' explicit multiplication question is recorded in the problem index. The ordinary flat, transverse delta-distribution example is elementary and already fits Bott's argument. Its general sufficient modern formulation uses disjoint opposing wavefront directions. Hörmander's original theorem was actually read: Theorem 2.5.10, pp. 126–127, gives the distribution product criterion; Theorem 2.5.11, p. 127, gives the corresponding restriction criterion (Hörmander, 1971). Item 69.6 is B for this later precise general development, while 69.7 is E for the tensor-coordinate example. No claim of necessity is attached to the sufficient wavefront condition.

### 70: coherent sheaves

Serre's original *Faisceaux algébriques cohérents* was read for the affine module–sheaf construction, localization, coherence, and affine acyclicity (Serre, 1955, chapter II). The corrections of scope matter: a principal open in an affine scheme remains affine but need not be affine space; a coordinate ring is a quotient of a polynomial ring rather than automatically the polynomial ring itself; and affine vanishing concerns coherent or suitably quasicoherent sheaves, not arbitrary sheaves. Finite generation and relation modules are stated under the Noetherian hypotheses actually needed by the argument. The incomplete converse-affineness line is F. Its missing content is not supplied by guessing a later theorem.

### 71: value distribution and characteristic forms

Finite-dimensional omission statements need the appropriate general-position and linear-nondegeneracy assumptions. The Sturm–Liouville endpoint-curve construction requires nonzero initial data such as \((0,1)\); zero Cauchy data yield the zero solution. The proposed Banach-space extension is too underspecified to identify as a theorem. The explicit Hilbert-space example below demonstrates why an unrestricted finite-omission analogue would fail; it does not refute an unstated spectral hypothesis. The request for a “satisfactory theory” of several-variable zero distributions is a research programme, not a definite unsolved problem.

For the characteristic-class material, Bott and Chern's 1965 paper was read in detail, especially §2 and Proposition 3.2 (Bott & Chern, 1965, pp. 75–81). A reciprocal coefficient and a genuine handwritten sign mistake are mathematical annotations, not transcription repairs.

### 73: curvature jets and historical classification

The finite-order question is: which algebraic arrays \((R,\nabla R,\ldots,\nabla^kR)\), at a point with a prescribed inner product, arise from a metric? Jentsch's Definition 1 and Theorem 3 were read for the precise modern compatibility conditions and realization statement (Jentsch, 2026, pp. 5–6, 10–11). This supplies the current mathematical answer, not its date of discovery. The checked document is revision 2, dated **13 January 2026**, of a preprint originally posted in 2015. Its abstract attributes the classical theorem to Cartan, and the introduction describes the realization theorem as a restatement of the classical jet-isomorphism theorem.

Item 73.5 is therefore E with **medium historical confidence**. Janyška's primary research article explicitly traces the replacement and curvature-reduction theorems to Thomas, Thomas and Michal, Veblen, and Schouten (Janyška, 2011, pp. 231–232). Thomas's 1926 publisher record was opened and confirms the publication and receipt dates; its original proof was not obtained. The full modern nonlinear identity list has consequently not been independently located word for word in a pre-1965 original. The catalogue distinguishes this bounded chronological uncertainty from the verified modern realization theorem. It does not infer an open minimal-presentation problem for scalar invariant rings from the notebook's broad wording.

The distinguished polynomial needs a constant term. Analytic and smooth preparation have different uniqueness behavior. The flat smooth example below proves nonuniqueness directly. The literal unqualified claim of at least \(2^n\) intersections is **false**. Gong's Theorem 1 and proof establish a directly checked nontransverse cup-length statement (Gong, 2023, pp. 50, 54–55). ### 75: Chern connections, algebraic cohomology, and polynomial normal forms

A Chern connection requires both a holomorphic structure and a Hermitian metric. Its type components are not two independent full connections. The metric pairing is sesquilinear, so the missing conjugation is substantive. In the column-frame convention \(Ds=\theta s\) the graded calculation gives \(\partial\theta=\theta^2\), and hence curvature of type \((1,1)\). The type of Chern forms alone does not imply a Hodge decomposition for every compact complex manifold.

Watts's complete two-page original paper was read (Watts, 1965, pp. 1027–1028). The construction uses a unital commutative algebra, \(p+1\) arguments in degree \(p\), insertion of a unit at all \(p+2\) positions in the differential, and the prescribed localization. It computes **cohomology**, not homology. The comparison with Alexander–Spanier cohomology is stated for compact Hausdorff spaces and real continuous-function algebras.

Their entries are explicitly unverified, while the one-coordinate canonical form was read in a later primary source, Lladser's equation (2) (Lladser, 2007, p. 513). Arnold's later discussion of the Whitney obstruction was read at p. 39 (Arnold, 1968). The notebook example has five factors; algebraic transcendence, rather than mere nonpolynomiality, is the relevant hypothesis. ### Hilbert-space omission example (71.4; proved here)

Let
\[
F(z)=\bigl(e^{nz}/n!\bigr)_{n\geq0}\in\ell^2(\mathbb C).
\]
On each compact set in \(z\), the squared norms of the coordinate series and all its derivative series are bounded by convergent factorial series, so \(F\) is an entire Hilbert-space-valued map. Every coordinate is nonzero. Thus it avoids every coordinate hyperplane, and every finite collection of those defining coordinate functionals is independent.

If a continuous linear functional with coefficient sequence \((a_n)\in\ell^2\) annihilates its image, then
\(G(w)=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nw^n/n!\) is entire by Cauchy–Schwarz and satisfies \(G(e^z)=0\). Since \(e^\mathbb C=\mathbb C^*\), the identity theorem gives \(G=0\), hence every \(a_n=0\). Hahn–Banach then shows that the closed linear span of the image is the whole Hilbert space. This rules out the unrestricted finite-omission analogue even for a linearly nondegenerate entire curve.

### Cusp and perturbed complex square (72.4–72.5; computed here)

For \(F(x_1,x_2)=(x_1,x_2^3-x_1x_2)\), the Jacobian determinant is \(3x_2^2-x_1\). The critical locus is \((3t^2,t)\) and its image is \((3t^2,-2t^3)\), giving \(27y_2^2=4y_1^3\). Away from zero these are folds. The scalar polynomial \(t^3-Ct\) is strictly increasing for \(C<0\) and has two turning points for \(C>0\).

For \(\varepsilon=\rho e^{i\theta}\ne0\), set \(z=\rho e^{i\theta/3}\zeta\) and divide the target by \(\rho^2e^{2i\theta/3}\). The map becomes \(\zeta^2+\bar\zeta\). Writing \(\zeta=x+iy\) gives real map \((x^2-y^2+x,2xy-y)\) and determinant \(4(x^2+y^2)-1\). The critical circle has image
\[
w(t)=\tfrac14e^{2it}+\tfrac12e^{-it}.
\]
Its derivative vanishes exactly when \(e^{3it}=1\). At each such point, with \(u=e^{-it}=e^{2it}\), one has \(w''=-3u/2\) and \(w'''=-3iu/2\); their real determinant is \(9/4\ne0\). There are three ordinary cusps and folds elsewhere on the critical circle.

### Three intersections on the two-torus (74.14; proved here)

On \(T^2=(\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z)^2\), put
\[
S(x,y)=\sin x+\sin y-\sin(x+y).
\]
The critical equations are \(\cos x=\cos y=\cos(x+y)\). The first equality gives \(x=y\) or \(x=-y\) modulo \(2\pi\). On the latter branch the only solution is \((0,0)\). On the former, \(c=\cos x\) satisfies \(c=2c^2-1\), so the complete critical set is
\[
(0,0),\quad(2\pi/3,2\pi/3),\quad(4\pi/3,4\pi/3).
\]
At zero the Hessian vanishes and
\(S=xy(x+y)/2+O(\|(x,y)\|^5)\).
At the other points the Hessian determinants are \(9/4\), with one positive-definite and one negative-definite Hessian. Hence the graph of \(\delta\,dS\) meets the zero section in exactly three distinct points, with a degenerate intersection at zero.

For completeness this graph is obtained by a **compactly supported Hamiltonian** in a fiber neighborhood. Use \(\lambda=p\,dq\), \(\omega=d\lambda\), and \(\iota_{X_H}\omega=-dH\), so \(\dot q=H_p\), \(\dot p=-H_q\). Let \(B\) be an open fiber neighborhood of zero and take \(\delta>0\) small enough that a closed ball of radius \(R>\delta\sup\|dS\|\) lies in \(B\). Choose \(\chi\in C_c^\infty(B)\) equal to one on that ball and put \(H(q,p)=-\delta S(q)\chi(p)\). The trajectory from \((q,0)\) is \((q,t\delta dS(q))\) for \(0\leq t\leq1\), since it stays where \(\chi=1\). The time-one map therefore sends the zero section to the stated graph. This example directly separates the degenerate lower bound three from the transverse bound four.

### Smooth-division nonuniqueness (74.10; proved here)

Take the nonzero smooth germ \(f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}\) for \(x\ne0\), extended by zero at \(x=0\), and set \(q(x,y)=f(x)/(x^2+y^2)\) away from the origin, extending it by zero at the origin. For any derivative order, differentiation gives a finite sum bounded by constants times \(|f^{(j)}(x)|r^{-N}\), where \(r=(x^2+y^2)^{1/2}\). Flatness gives \(|f^{(j)}(x)|\leq C|x|^{N+L}\leq Cr^{N+L}\) for arbitrary \(L\). Thus every derivative extends and vanishes to arbitrary order. The identities \(f=(x^2+y^2)0+f\) and \(f=(x^2+y^2)q+0\) give distinct smooth divisions with remainders of degree less than two in \(y\).

### Chern-connection sign (75.7; proved here)

With \(\theta=\partial N\,N^{-1}\), the graded product rule gives
\[
\partial\theta=-\partial N\wedge\partial(N^{-1})
=\partial N\,N^{-1}\wedge\partial N\,N^{-1}=\theta^2.
\]
Consequently \(K=d\theta-\theta^2=\bar\partial\theta\) in the stated convention. The differentiated minus sign visible in the notebook misses the second minus from \(\partial N^{-1}=-N^{-1}(\partial N)N^{-1}\). The corresponding Bianchi identity is \(dK=\theta K-K\theta\).

### Regular orbits and the counterexample to the boxed theorem (76.5–76.7; proved here)

For commuting divergence-free fields \(a,b\) on an oriented three-manifold with volume form \(\mu\), let \(\alpha=\iota_b\iota_a\mu\). Cartan's formula and commutation imply \(d\alpha=0\). If \(H^1(M;\mathbb R)=0\), write \(\alpha=dF\). At a point where \(a,b\) are independent, \(dF\ne0\), and they span the tangent plane to the regular level. On a compact connected boundaryless component \(C\) consisting of such points, the complete commuting flows give a transitive \(\mathbb R^2\)-action. Its stabilizer is discrete and cocompact, hence a rank-two lattice; therefore \(C\cong T^2\). A one-parameter subgroup can still have irrational slope and dense, nonclosed individual trajectories.

For the separate unqualified notebook claim, take the round unit \(S^3\subset\mathbb C^2\), set \(a(z_1,z_2)=(iz_1,i\gamma z_2)\) with irrational \(\gamma\), and set \(b=0\). Both fields are divergence-free, they commute, and \(H^1(S^3;\mathbb R)=0\). At \(z_1z_2\ne0\), the joint \(\mathbb R^2\)-orbit is the irrational trajectory of \(a\), dense and proper in its Clifford torus, so it is not closed or compact. The joint action has rank one and \(F\) is constant, precisely the excluded situation. Compactness of the ambient manifold alone does not make an orbit compact.

### Conditional viscosity estimate, Hamiltonian spectrum, and affine metric (77.3, 77.5, 77.10; proved or computed here)

Suppose a steady solution \(u_*\) is already known for a fixed force and no-slip bounded domain. For \(w=v-u_*\), the divergence-free transport cancellations give
\[
\tfrac12\frac d{dt}\|w\|_2^2+\nu\|\nabla w\|_2^2
=-\int (w\cdot\nabla)u_*\cdot w.
\]
With Poincaré constant \(\lambda_1>0\), the condition \(\|\nabla u_*\|_\infty<\nu\lambda_1\) gives exponential decay at rate at least \(2(\nu\lambda_1-\|\nabla u_*\|_\infty)\) for the squared norm on the sufficiently regular solution's interval of existence. Here the gradient bound uses the pointwise matrix operator norm, and both solutions have the same homogeneous no-slip boundary conditions. This is conditional stability; it proves neither existence of a steady solution nor global classical existence of the evolution.

If \(A=JH\) is a real Hamiltonian matrix with invertible skew-symmetric \(J\) and symmetric \(H\), then \(A^T=-HJ=J^{-1}(-A)J\). Its spectrum is therefore symmetric under negation, and reality adds complex conjugation. Eigenvalues occur in the usual quartets with possible coincidences. Infinite-dimensional versions require additional spectral hypotheses.

For the orientation-preserving affine group with law \((b,a)(b',a')=(b+ab',aa')\), \(a>0\), the left-invariant metric that is Euclidean at the identity is \((db^2+da^2)/a^2\). Writing its conformal factor as \(u=-\log a\), the Gaussian curvature formula \(K=-e^{-2u}\Delta u\) gives \(K=-1\). This explicit example is independently computed; it is not claimed to have been located verbatim in Arnold's paper.
